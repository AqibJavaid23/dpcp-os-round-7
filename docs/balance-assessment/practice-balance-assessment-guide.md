BALANCE ASSESSMENT
GUIDE
How to conduct, interpret, and act on a complete practice balance assessment
| What this guide covers /  / What the balance assessment is and why it works / How to run the assessment step by step / How to calculate every metric and where to find the data / How to interpret every result and what to do when a metric is off target / The sequence rule — which imbalances to act on first |

# WHAT IS THE BALANCE ASSESSMENT?
A dental practice generates revenue through a sequence of connected events. Patients arrive, get examined, receive a treatment recommendation, accept that treatment, have it scheduled, and the practice collects payment. When the practice is underperforming, the problem is almost never the practice as a whole — it is one specific link in that sequence that is broken or underperforming.
The balance assessment is a structured diagnostic tool that evaluates every link in that sequence through ratios. Rather than looking at total revenue or total collections as a single number, it compares specific parts of the practice against each other. The comparison — the ratio — reveals where imbalance exists. Imbalance is where the growth opportunity lives.
| The core principle / Every metric in this assessment is compared against another related metric. A practice with 300 new patients per month and zero hygiene reappointments has a patient flow number that looks strong — until you compare it to the retention rate and realize the practice is churning its entire patient base every year. Ratios reveal what raw numbers hide. |

The assessment has five parts, ordered from highest to lowest impact. The parts are not independent — they form a pipeline. Patient flow feeds treatment conversion. Treatment conversion feeds schedule efficiency. Each part depends on the parts above it working correctly.
This is why the sequence matters. If you find an imbalance in Part 1 — patient flow — that imbalance is the entire priority. You do not optimize treatment conversion while the practice does not have enough patients to convert. You do not work on accounts receivable while conversion is broken. You find the highest point of imbalance and fix it first.
# HOW TO RUN THE ASSESSMENT
## Before you begin
Pull the following data from your practice management software for the most recent full month. Use a full calendar month — not a partial month or a week. Partial periods produce misleading ratios.
Total new patients seen
Total exams completed, broken out by doctor
Total hygiene visits completed
Doctor days worked per doctor
Hygiene days worked
Active patient base count
Active patients with a future hygiene appointment already booked
Hygiene reappointment rate (most practice management systems report this directly)
Total production and total collections
Doctor production broken out by doctor
Hygiene production
Total treatment presented and total treatment accepted, broken out by doctor
Total AR balance and breakdown by aging bucket
Insurance AR vs. patient AR
| Where to find the data / Most practice management systems (Dentrix, Eaglesoft, Curve, Open Dental) can produce all of these numbers from their standard reporting modules. Look for: Production & Collections Report, AR Aging Report, Hygiene Reappointment Report, New Patient Report, and Provider Production Detail. If your software does not produce one of these reports directly, ask your software support team — all of these data points exist in every modern system. |

## Running the assessment
Work through each part in order. Fill in the practice value for every metric. Compare it to the target. Mark each metric with the appropriate status: check mark if at or above target, exclamation if within 10% of target, cross if below target.
After completing all five parts, return to the summary page. The summary page shows you at a glance where the highest-priority imbalances sit. The first part with a failing metric is your starting point for intervention.
Do not skip parts or jump ahead to later sections because they seem more interesting or more urgent. The sequence is the discipline. A practice that is struggling in Part 3 almost always has a Part 1 or Part 2 problem that is causing it.
| How often to run the assessment / Monthly. The assessment is most useful as a month-over-month tracking tool, not a one-time snapshot. Run it on the same day each month using the previous month's data. Over time, the trend on each metric tells you more than any single month's result. |

| PART 1 / PATIENT FLOW / The ceiling on everything else. If this section shows imbalance, it is the only priority until resolved. |

Patient flow measures whether the practice has enough patients moving through the right channels to support its providers. The most expensive problem in any dental practice is a provider sitting in a chair with no patient in it. Every other optimization is secondary to ensuring that providers are busy with patients who have been properly prepared to receive and accept care.
This section also measures whether the practice is retaining the patients it already has. A practice can have strong new patient numbers and still be shrinking if it is not retaining its existing patient base on a proper recall cycle.
1.1  New Patients per Doctor per Month
| How to calculate | New patients seen in the month ÷ number of active full-time doctors. A full-time doctor works 4 days per week, approximately 16 days per month. Part-time doctors should be prorated. | Target | 30–40 per full-time doctor |

This is the primary indicator of whether the practice is growing its patient base. Below 20 new patients per doctor per month, the practice is not generating enough volume to sustain growth. In multi-doctor practices, measure this per doctor — a blended average can hide that one doctor is being significantly under-fed.
| ON TARGET / 30–40 new patients per doctor | MONITOR / 20–29 new patients — below sustainable pace | ACTION REQUIRED / Below 20 — critical growth risk | IF RED — WHAT TO DO / Review marketing channel performance. Check whether the practice is accepting new patients on the insurance panels that your market uses. Evaluate whether new hygiene appointment availability (1.6) is limiting new patient bookings. |

1.2  Exams per Doctor per Month
| How to calculate | Total exams completed in the month ÷ number of active doctors. Measure per doctor, not as a blended practice total. | Target | 120–150 exams per full-time doctor |

Exams are the gateway to all doctor production. A doctor who is not completing enough exams is not diagnosing enough treatment. In a multi-doctor practice, this metric must be reviewed per doctor — if one doctor is doing 140 exams and another is doing 60, the problem is allocation, not overall volume.
| ON TARGET / 120–150 exams per doctor | MONITOR / 90–119 exams — monitor; check hygiene feeder | ACTION REQUIRED / Below 90 — doctor is underutilized | IF RED — WHAT TO DO / Check the doctor-to-hygiene days ratio (1.4). A doctor not getting enough exams is usually not being fed enough by hygiene. Also check whether the exam split between providers is equitable (1.3). |

1.3  Exam Split Between Providers
| How to calculate | Exams completed by Doctor A divided by exams completed by Doctor B. Normalize against actual days worked before comparing. If Doctor A worked 18 days and Doctor B worked 12, adjust accordingly. | Target | Within 15–20% of each other |

In a multi-doctor practice, if exams are not being distributed roughly equally between providers, one doctor will be overproducing while the other underproduces. This creates retention risk for the overworked doctor and financial loss from the underworked one. The root cause is almost always scheduling — one doctor is being preferentially booked.
| ON TARGET / Within 15% variance when normalized for days | MONITOR / 15–25% variance — review scheduling practice | ACTION REQUIRED / Over 25% variance — scheduling is imbalanced | IF RED — WHAT TO DO / Examine how new patients and recall patients are being assigned to each doctor's schedule. If one doctor is receiving significantly more patient flow, adjust scheduling protocols to distribute equitably. |

1.4  Doctor Days to Hygiene Days Ratio
| How to calculate | Total doctor days scheduled in the month ÷ total hygiene days scheduled in the month. A full-time doctor works approximately 16 days per month. | Target | 1 doctor day for every 2 hygiene days |

Hygiene is the engine that feeds the doctor. Every hygiene appointment is a patient who will receive an exam, and that exam becomes the source of the doctor's restorative production. If hygiene capacity is below this ratio, the doctor will not have enough examined patients to sustain their production level. This is one of the most common hidden causes of doctor underproduction.
| ON TARGET / Ratio at or above 1:2 | MONITOR / Ratio between 1:1.5 and 1:2 — hygiene is tight | ACTION REQUIRED / Ratio below 1:1.5 — hygiene is severely underpowered | IF RED — WHAT TO DO / Evaluate whether hygiene staffing is adequate. Consider adding hygiene days before adding marketing spend — the bottleneck is capacity to see patients, not awareness. Also check new hygiene appointment availability (1.6) to confirm the capacity problem. |

1.5  Active Patients Current on Recare
| How to calculate | Number of active patients with a future hygiene appointment already booked ÷ total active patient base. | Target | 70% or more |

This is the patient retention metric. A practice with 2,000 active patients where only 900 have a future appointment booked is operating with 55% of its patient base unscheduled. Those unscheduled patients are at risk of drifting to another practice or simply not returning. This metric reveals whether the recall system is functioning as a retention engine.
| ON TARGET / 70%+ on recare | MONITOR / 60–69% — retention system is leaking | ACTION REQUIRED / Below 60% — significant patient base is at risk | IF RED — WHAT TO DO / Review the reappointment process at the hygiene chair. Patients should leave every hygiene appointment with their next appointment already booked. If they are not, the hygiene reappointment rate (1.7) will also be failing. These two metrics move together. |

1.6  New Hygiene Appointment Availability
| How to calculate | Call or check the scheduling system as if you are a new patient. Count how many days until you can be offered 3 available hygiene appointment options. | Target | 3 openings available within 1–2 weeks |

If a new patient cannot be offered 3 appointment options within two weeks, the practice is throttling its own new patient flow. Prospective patients who are told the first available appointment is four or five weeks away frequently call the next practice on their list. This metric identifies when the constraint on new patient growth is capacity, not marketing.
| ON TARGET / 3 openings within 2 weeks | MONITOR / 3 openings in 2–3 weeks — approaching capacity ceiling | ACTION REQUIRED / 3 openings more than 3 weeks out — capacity is limiting growth | IF RED — WHAT TO DO / If hygiene is booked beyond 3 weeks, the practice needs additional hygiene capacity before investing further in new patient marketing. Adding a hygiene day before a new patient marketing campaign will produce a higher return. |

1.7  Hygiene Reappointment Rate
| How to calculate | Patients who leave a hygiene visit with a future hygiene appointment already booked ÷ total hygiene patients seen in the month. | Target | 90% or higher |

Every hygiene patient who leaves without a future appointment is a patient the practice has to chase. A reappointment rate below 80% means the hygiene department is functionally leaking its own patient base — generating visits without securing the next one. This directly impacts the active patients current on recare metric (1.5).
| ON TARGET / 90%+ | MONITOR / 80–89% — some patients are leaving unscheduled | ACTION REQUIRED / Below 80% — reappointment process is broken | IF RED — WHAT TO DO / Retrain the hygiene team on the pre-appointment conversation. The next appointment should be discussed and booked before the patient moves to checkout, not at the front desk. Booking at the chair has a significantly higher success rate than booking at checkout. |

1.8  New Patient Reappointment Rate
| How to calculate | New patients who book a second appointment before leaving ÷ total new patients seen in the month. | Target | 75% or higher |

A new patient who leaves without a future appointment represents a significant acquisition cost with no guaranteed return. The first visit establishes the relationship — the second visit confirms retention. A rate below 65% suggests that new patients are not being converted into long-term patients of the practice.
| ON TARGET / 75%+ | MONITOR / 65–74% — some new patients are not being retained | ACTION REQUIRED / Below 65% — new patient conversion is failing | IF RED — WHAT TO DO / Ensure every new patient exam includes a specific conversation about the next visit before the patient reaches checkout. The doctor and hygienist should set the expectation for the recall appointment during the visit, not at the door. |

1.9  No-Show and Cancellation Rate
| How to calculate | Total appointments not kept in the month ÷ total appointments scheduled in the month. | Target | Below 10% |

High no-show and cancellation rates are not just a scheduling inconvenience — they represent direct production loss from chairs that were committed and then went unused. Above 15% indicates either the wrong patients are being scheduled, confirmation protocols are not working, or the schedule is being managed reactively rather than proactively.
| ON TARGET / Below 10% | MONITOR / 10–15% — monitor confirmation process | ACTION REQUIRED / Above 15% — significant production loss | IF RED — WHAT TO DO / Implement a same-day confirmation protocol and a structured short-call list for filling cancellations. Review whether the practice's patient demographic has a structural cancellation tendency that requires adjustments to the confirmation cadence. |

| PART 2 / TREATMENT CONVERSION / Assumes patient flow is adequate. Measures how much of the treatment being diagnosed is being presented, accepted, and scheduled. |

Treatment conversion is where most of the latent revenue in an established practice lives. The same patient, in the same chair, with the same dentist, can generate vastly different production depending on how treatment is presented and closed. This section evaluates whether the practice is realizing the full clinical and financial value of the patient visits it is already generating.
All metrics in this section must be measured per doctor. Blended averages across multiple doctors hide individual performance gaps that require different interventions. A practice where one doctor converts at 75% and another at 35% has a coaching problem with a specific individual — not a practice-wide systems problem.
2.1  Patient Acceptance Rate
| How to calculate | Patients who accept any treatment recommendation ÷ total patients who receive a treatment recommendation. Measure per doctor. | Target | 75–80% |

This measures whether the patient said yes to anything. It is a trust and communication metric. A patient who leaves with no treatment accepted either had nothing diagnosed, rejected the recommendation entirely, or was not presented with a compelling enough reason to act. Below 65% for any individual doctor is a signal that the patient communication style, presentation approach, or case-building method needs to change.
| ON TARGET / 75–80% | MONITOR / 65–74% — some patients are leaving without any treatment | ACTION REQUIRED / Below 65% — presentation or trust issue | IF RED — WHAT TO DO / Review the doctor's case presentation approach. The recommendation needs to connect the clinical finding to a consequence the patient understands and cares about. Training on patient communication and case presentation is often more impactful than any operational change. |

2.2  Treatment Dollar Acceptance Rate
| How to calculate | Dollar value of treatment accepted ÷ dollar value of treatment presented. Measure per doctor. | Target | 35–50% |

This is a different question from 2.1. A patient can say yes to a filling and no to a crown — they accepted treatment but rejected a large portion of the diagnosed dollar value. This metric tells you how much of what was presented is actually being captured. It identifies whether the problem is in small cases being rejected (a trust issue) or large cases being rejected (a financing or urgency issue).
| ON TARGET / 35–50% | MONITOR / 25–34% — significant diagnosed revenue not converting | ACTION REQUIRED / Below 25% — major revenue leak in conversion | IF RED — WHAT TO DO / If large cases are being rejected at a high rate, examine whether financing options are being presented on every case above a defined dollar threshold. Patients who decline treatment because of perceived cost often accept the same treatment when offered a monthly payment option. |

2.3  Treatment Accepted per Exam
| How to calculate | Dollar value of treatment accepted ÷ number of exams completed. Measure per doctor. | Target | $300–500 per exam |

This is the conversion efficiency metric — it collapses diagnosis, presentation, and acceptance into a single number per provider. Two doctors seeing the same number of patients but producing different accepted treatment per exam have different problems. Compare this metric to 2.4 to diagnose the specific failure point.
| ON TARGET / $300–500 per exam | MONITOR / $200–299 — below expected conversion efficiency | ACTION REQUIRED / Below $200 — significant underperformance | IF RED — WHAT TO DO / First compare to metric 2.4 (treatment presented per exam). If 2.4 is also low, the doctor is under-diagnosing or under-presenting. If 2.4 is healthy but 2.3 is low, the issue is conversion — patients are hearing the recommendation but not accepting it. |

2.4  Treatment Presented per Exam
| How to calculate | Dollar value of treatment presented ÷ number of exams completed. Measure per doctor. | Target | $400–600 per exam |

This metric isolates the diagnosis and presentation step. If this number is low, the doctor is either not finding treatment (under-diagnosing) or not presenting what they find (withholding recommendations). Both are clinical and financial problems. A doctor who consistently presents below $200 per exam is not examining patients to a full standard of care.
| ON TARGET / $400–600 per exam | MONITOR / $250–399 — diagnosis or presentation is conservative | ACTION REQUIRED / Below $250 — significant under-diagnosis or under-presentation | IF RED — WHAT TO DO / If treatment presented is consistently low, review the doctor's examination protocol and clinical documentation standards. Compare to other providers in the same market to calibrate whether this is a practice-specific pattern or a market-wide characteristic. |

| PART 3 / SCHEDULE EFFICIENCY & PROVIDER CAPACITY / Assumes flow and conversion are healthy. Measures whether providers are producing at the ceiling of their available schedule. |

Once patient flow is adequate and treatment is being properly presented and accepted, the question becomes whether the available schedule time is being used to its maximum productive potential. This section also identifies whether the practice is approaching a capacity ceiling that will require adding a provider.
3.1  Doctor Production per Hour
| How to calculate | Doctor collections for the month ÷ total doctor chair hours worked. A full-time doctor (4 days/week, 8 hours/day) works approximately 139 hours per month. Measure per doctor. | Target | $550/hr floor  |  $700–800/hr healthy  |  $1,000/hr+ elite |

This is the single most important provider productivity metric. It tells you whether the schedule is being filled with the right types of appointments, and whether the doctor is working efficiently through those appointments. A doctor at $350/hr with a full schedule is doing low-value procedures or is not converting accepted treatment into scheduled appointments. A doctor at $800/hr with an empty schedule needs more patients — the bottleneck is Part 1, not this section.
| ON TARGET / $700+/hr | MONITOR / $550–699/hr — at floor; room to grow | ACTION REQUIRED / Below $550/hr — production efficiency problem | IF RED — WHAT TO DO / Examine the procedure mix. If the schedule is heavily weighted toward short, low-value appointments, adjust scheduling protocols to protect time blocks for restorative work. Review whether accepted treatment is being scheduled promptly or sitting as unscheduled accepted treatment. |

3.2  Restorative Appointment Availability
| How to calculate | Check each doctor's schedule: how many days until 3 openings for a crown-level appointment can be offered to a patient? Measure per doctor. | Target | 3 openings available within 1–2 weeks per doctor |

This metric identifies whether the doctor's restorative schedule is at capacity. If a patient who needs a crown cannot be seen within two weeks, the practice is delaying accepted treatment — which increases the risk of the patient failing to return. In a multi-doctor practice, this is measured separately per doctor. One doctor booked four weeks out while another has immediate availability is a scheduling allocation problem.
| ON TARGET / 3 openings within 2 weeks | MONITOR / 3 openings in 2–3 weeks — approaching restorative capacity | ACTION REQUIRED / 3 openings more than 3 weeks out — capacity is restricting accepted treatment | IF RED — WHAT TO DO / Protect restorative time blocks in the schedule. Do not allow hygiene re-care appointments or short procedures to fill restorative blocks. If a doctor is consistently booked more than 3 weeks out for restorative work, evaluate whether a second provider is needed. |

3.3  Hygiene as a Percentage of Total Production
| How to calculate | Total hygiene collections for the month ÷ total practice collections for the month. | Target | 20–30% |

This ratio is the balance gauge between the two production engines of the practice. Hygiene and the doctor should be producing in balance. If hygiene is below 20% of total production, the hygiene department is underdeveloped relative to the doctor — the practice is over-reliant on doctor production, which creates fragility when the doctor has a slow month. If hygiene is above 30%, the doctor is not converting the treatment that hygiene is diagnosing and delivering — the problem is in Part 2.
| ON TARGET / 20–30% hygiene | MONITOR / Below 20% — hygiene underdeveloped; above 30% — doctor underproducing | ACTION REQUIRED / Extreme variance from the 20–30% band | IF RED — WHAT TO DO / If below 20%: evaluate hygiene staffing and scheduling. If above 30%: return to Part 2 and review treatment conversion metrics per doctor. This metric cannot be interpreted without context from the sections above it. |

| PART 4 / HYGIENE PRODUCTION QUALITY / Measures whether the hygiene department is performing to clinical and production standards, not just filling chairs. |

Hygiene is the internal engine that sustains every other part of the practice. A hygiene department that is diagnosing at a high clinical standard, attaching the appropriate preventive services, and producing at its capacity ceiling creates a steady stream of treatment-ready patients for the doctor. A hygiene department that is under-diagnosing or under-attaching is suppressing the entire practice's production without it being visible in the topline numbers.
4.1  Perio Percentage
| How to calculate | Periodontal treatment visits (any non-prophy hygiene visit including scaling and root planing, periodontal maintenance) ÷ total hygiene visits in the month. | Target | 17–20% |

In any established patient base, roughly one in five hygiene visits should be a periodontal appointment rather than a routine prophy. Periodontal disease is endemic in the adult population. A hygiene department where less than 10% of visits are perio appointments is systematically under-diagnosing — which means patients with active disease are not being treated, and the practice is leaving significant production on the table. Perio visits produce two to three times the revenue of a standard prophy.
| ON TARGET / 17–20% perio | MONITOR / 10–16% — under-diagnosing likely | ACTION REQUIRED / Below 10% — systemic under-diagnosis | IF RED — WHAT TO DO / Conduct a clinical audit of the hygiene department's examination and documentation protocols. Review periodontal charting standards. Consider whether the hygiene team has been trained to present periodontal findings in a way that patients understand and accept. |

4.2  Fluoride Percentage
| How to calculate | Fluoride treatments delivered ÷ total hygiene visits in the month. | Target | 45–50% |

Fluoride is a standard of care recommendation for the majority of adult dental patients. A hygiene department delivering fluoride on fewer than 30% of visits is consistently under-attaching a service that belongs on most adult appointments. This is not a sales issue — it is a clinical standard of care issue. The revenue impact is significant: a practice doing 300 hygiene visits per month at 45% fluoride attachment versus 20% attachment is generating 75 additional fluoride codes per month.
| ON TARGET / 45–50% | MONITOR / 30–44% — inconsistent attachment | ACTION REQUIRED / Below 30% — fluoride is being routinely omitted | IF RED — WHAT TO DO / Retrain hygienists on fluoride recommendation protocols. Build fluoride into the standard hygiene visit workflow rather than treating it as an optional add-on. Track fluoride percentage by individual hygienist to identify whether the gap is practice-wide or specific to one team member. |

4.3  Hygiene Production per Day
| How to calculate | Total hygiene collections for the month ÷ total hygiene days worked in the month. | Target | $1,200–1,600/day (assisted hygienist)  |  $800–1,000/day (unassisted) |

Hygiene production per day is the most direct single-number indicator of overall hygiene department performance. It captures schedule density, procedure mix, and clinical standard of care in one figure. A hygienist consistently below $700/day is either under-scheduled, under-diagnosing, or missing the ancillary attachments that belong on each visit.
| ON TARGET / $1,200+/day assisted, $800+/day unassisted | MONITOR / Within 15% of target — monitor | ACTION REQUIRED / Below target threshold — hygiene underperforming | IF RED — WHAT TO DO / Identify whether the issue is scheduling (are hygiene chairs consistently full?), clinical (perio and fluoride percentages from 4.1 and 4.2 will indicate this), or a combination. Address the scheduling constraint before coaching on clinical standards. |

4.4  Hygiene Production per Hour
| How to calculate | Total hygiene collections for the month ÷ total hygiene chair hours worked in the month. | Target | $200–250/hr (assisted)  |  $150–200/hr (unassisted) |

The per-hour metric normalizes for hygienists working different schedule lengths. It allows direct comparison between full-time and part-time hygienists on an equal basis. Combined with 4.3, it creates a complete picture of hygiene efficiency — a hygienist could be at the per-day target by working longer hours while still underperforming on an hourly basis.
| ON TARGET / $200+/hr assisted, $150+/hr unassisted | MONITOR / Within 15% of target — monitor | ACTION REQUIRED / Below target threshold — efficiency issue | IF RED — WHAT TO DO / Compare to 4.3. If per-day is healthy but per-hour is low, the hygienist may be working longer-than-standard appointments. Review appointment length protocols and whether extended appointment times are clinically justified or a scheduling inefficiency. |

| PART 5 / ACCOUNTS RECEIVABLE / Measures whether what the practice produced is being collected. This section recovers existing revenue — it does not generate new revenue. |

By the time a problem appears in the accounts receivable metrics, clinical care has already been delivered and the production has already been recorded. AR management does not increase what a practice produces — it determines what percentage of that production the practice actually keeps. A practice with strong production and poor AR management can be cash-poor despite excellent clinical performance.
5.1  Collections Rate
| How to calculate | Total collections for the month ÷ total production adjusted for contractual write-offs (production minus expected insurance adjustments). | Target | 95% or higher |

The collections rate is the foundational AR metric. Everything else in this section explains why the collections rate is what it is. A rate below 88% means the practice is losing more than 12 cents of every dollar it produces — which at $300,000/month in production represents over $36,000 per month in uncollected revenue. This is a billing problem, a patient responsibility collection problem, or both.
| ON TARGET / 95%+ | MONITOR / 90–94% — some leakage; investigate | ACTION REQUIRED / Below 90% — significant revenue loss; billing audit required | IF RED — WHAT TO DO / Conduct a billing audit to identify where the gap is occurring. Compare insurance AR aging to patient AR aging (metric 5.5) to determine whether the problem is on the insurance collection side or the patient payment side. The two require different interventions. |

5.2  Total AR to Monthly Production Ratio
| How to calculate | Total outstanding AR balance ÷ average monthly production. | Target | Below 1:1 |

If a practice produces $200,000 per month and carries $350,000 in total AR, it has 1.75 months of production sitting uncollected. A healthy practice should have total AR that clears within roughly one production cycle. Above 1:1 means either old claims are not being worked, patient balances are not being collected, or both.
| ON TARGET / Below 1:1 | MONITOR / 1:1 to 1.5:1 — elevated; investigate aging | ACTION REQUIRED / Above 1.5:1 — significant AR backlog | IF RED — WHAT TO DO / Review AR aging by bucket (current, 30, 60, 90, 90+). The problem is almost always concentrated in the 90+ days bucket. Focus collection efforts there first — old AR has a significantly lower recovery rate than current AR. |

5.3  AR Over 90 Days
| How to calculate | AR balance 90 days or more past the date of service ÷ total AR balance. | Target | Below 10% |

AR that has aged past 90 days becomes increasingly difficult to collect. Insurance companies have appeal deadlines that may have passed. Patients who have not paid within 90 days are significantly less likely to pay voluntarily. A practice allowing more than 15% of its AR to age past 90 days is systematically writing off revenue that was earned and never recovered.
| ON TARGET / Below 10% | MONITOR / 10–15% — aging is accumulating | ACTION REQUIRED / Above 15% — recovery rate will be significantly impaired | IF RED — WHAT TO DO / Implement a weekly AR follow-up protocol specifically targeting the 60–90 day aging bucket before claims age past 90 days. Establish a defined escalation process for claims that reach 90 days without resolution. |

5.4  Days in AR
| How to calculate | Total AR balance ÷ average daily production (monthly production ÷ working days in the month). | Target | Under 30 days |

Days in AR translates the total AR balance into a time-based metric that any team member can understand intuitively. If the practice produces $10,000 per working day and has $450,000 in AR, it has 45 days of production sitting uncollected. Above 45 days indicates a meaningful billing lag that is creating cash flow pressure even when production is strong.
| ON TARGET / Under 30 days | MONITOR / 30–45 days — elevated; monitor | ACTION REQUIRED / Above 45 days — billing lag is impacting cash flow | IF RED — WHAT TO DO / Review the speed of claims submission. Claims should be submitted within 24–48 hours of the date of service. Delayed submission is the single most common cause of elevated days in AR. |

5.5  Insurance vs. Patient AR Split
| How to calculate | Insurance AR balance ÷ patient AR balance. This is a diagnostic ratio — there is no single pass/fail target. | Target | Identify which side is driving the aging |

This metric does not have a universal target because the right ratio depends on the practice's payer mix. Its value is diagnostic. If most of the aging AR sits on the insurance side, the problem is claims — submission, follow-up, or coding. If most aging AR sits on the patient side, the problem is the patient collections conversation at checkout or payment plan management. These are completely different problems that require different solutions.
| ON TARGET / Aging balanced or insurance-heavy with active follow-up | MONITOR / One side disproportionately high — investigate | ACTION REQUIRED / Patient side aging significantly — collections process has failed | IF RED — WHAT TO DO / If insurance AR is aging: audit claim submission protocols and denial response rates. If patient AR is aging: review the payment and financial arrangements conversation happening at checkout. Ensure payment expectations are set clearly at the time of treatment. |

# THE SEQUENCE RULE
The most important thing to understand about the balance assessment is not any individual metric — it is the order in which imbalances must be addressed.
The five parts of this assessment form a pipeline. Patient flow feeds treatment conversion. Treatment conversion feeds schedule efficiency and provider capacity. Hygiene production quality operates in parallel with Parts 1 through 3 but depends on patient flow being healthy first. Accounts receivable captures the revenue that all of the above generates.
| The rule / When you complete the assessment and identify the first part that has a failing metric, that part is the sole intervention priority. Do not work on any lower part until the higher imbalance has a documented resolution plan in place and measurable progress has been made. |

A practice cannot convert its way out of a patient flow problem. Adding a treatment coordinator when the practice does not have enough patients to examine will not move the numbers. A practice cannot collect its way out of a conversion problem — improving billing efficiency when treatment acceptance is at 25% does not address the root cause of the revenue shortfall.
The sequence also tells you how to interpret a metric that looks bad. If hygiene production per day is below target, the first question is whether patient flow is healthy. If the hygiene schedule is thin because there are not enough patients, the hygiene production number is a symptom of Part 1 — not an independent Part 4 problem. Treating it as a Part 4 problem and coaching the hygienist on perio diagnosis will have no effect if she does not have patients to see.
| PART 1 / Patient Flow /  / HIGHEST PRIORITY | PART 2 / Treatment Conversion | PART 3 / Schedule Efficiency & Capacity | PART 4 / Hygiene Quality | PART 5 / Accounts Receivable /  / LOWEST PRIORITY |

The sequence runs left to right. The first column with a failing metric is the only intervention. Everything to the right of it is secondary until the higher-priority imbalance is resolved.
# COMMON IMBALANCE PATTERNS
After running this assessment across many practices, certain patterns appear repeatedly. Understanding these patterns allows a practice manager to move faster from diagnosis to resolution.
## Pattern 1 — Strong production, weak collections
The doctor is busy, the schedule is full, and monthly production numbers look good. But cash flow is tight and the bank account does not reflect the production figures. This almost always appears in Part 5 — AR over 90 days is high, days in AR is elevated, and the insurance-to-patient split reveals where the backlog is sitting. The fix is a billing process audit, not a production change.
## Pattern 2 — Full schedule, low production per hour
The practice is booked out and the schedule looks productive, but doctor production per hour is below $550. This usually means the schedule is full of short, low-fee appointments while larger restorative cases are being deferred or not presented. The imbalance shows up in Part 3 but the root cause is in Part 2 — treatment presented per exam and treatment dollar acceptance rate will both be low, indicating that large cases are being under-presented or under-closed.
## Pattern 3 — Low new patients, good retention
The active patient base is healthy and recare percentages are strong, but new patient counts are below 20 per doctor. This is a marketing and awareness problem, not an internal systems problem. The hygiene appointment availability metric (1.6) should be checked first — if hygiene is booked more than 3 weeks out, capacity is limiting new patient acquisition. If availability is good, the issue is external marketing reach.
## Pattern 4 — Multi-doctor imbalance
Two doctors in the same practice with very different exam counts, production per hour, or treatment acceptance rates. The blended practice metrics look acceptable, but one provider is significantly outperforming the other. This pattern requires per-doctor analysis — the underperforming doctor needs a specific intervention, whether scheduling, coaching, or a different case mix.
## Pattern 5 — High cancellation, low reappointment
No-show and cancellation rates above 15% combined with hygiene reappointment rates below 80%. This is a patient commitment and scheduling management problem. The practice is not securing commitments at the chair and is not managing its short-call list proactively. The result is a schedule that looks full in the morning and has holes by afternoon.
# RUNNING THE ASSESSMENT AS A TEAM
The balance assessment is most effective when it is run collaboratively by the practice manager and the clinical team leads. The practice manager owns the data pull and the ratio calculations. The doctor or doctors own the interpretation of the clinical metrics — particularly the treatment presentation and conversion ratios, which reflect individual provider behavior.
Present the results in a structured monthly review. Start with the summary page. Identify the highest-priority imbalance. Build a specific, time-bound resolution plan before leaving the meeting. Set a review date — typically 30 days — to assess whether the intervention is working before moving to the next imbalance.
| The goal of the assessment / The assessment does not produce a list of 20 things to fix. It produces one priority. Every month, one priority. The team that builds the discipline of identifying the highest imbalance and addressing only that imbalance until it is resolved will consistently outperform teams that generate long improvement lists and act on none of them completely. |

Over time, a practice that runs this assessment monthly will build a clear picture of how each metric moves in response to specific interventions. That history becomes the practice's operating intelligence — a record of what worked, what did not, and what the practice's natural performance ceiling looks like for each metric in its specific market and patient demographic.
The numbers do not lie. Run them consistently. Act on what they tell you. Measure the response. Repeat.
